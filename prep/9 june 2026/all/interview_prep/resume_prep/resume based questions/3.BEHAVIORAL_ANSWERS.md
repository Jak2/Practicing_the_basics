# BEHAVIORAL ANSWERS — Fully Scripted

Learn the *shape* of each answer, then say it in your own words. Behavioral answers use **STAR** (Situation, Task, Action, Result) — but keep them tight, 60–90 seconds, and always end on a result. Be honest, positive, and never bad-mouth anyone.

---
# THE OPENER

⚠️ **Seniority signal:** Open with impact and ownership, not tech stack. "I architected..." beats "I used FastAPI to build..." every time. End on metrics. Stop at 2 minutes.

**Q. Tell me about yourself.** *(your single most important answer — rehearse most)*
"I'm a backend Python engineer with around five-plus years building production systems. My core has always been API design, databases, and async architecture. At Nokia I spent nearly three years as an automation and Python engineer — I architected NFTRACE, a distributed tracing platform built on FastAPI and Redis that cut our pipeline validation time in half, and I mentored engineers and authored code-review standards the team adopted. Then I built a 5G failure-remediation pipeline that took log analysis from hours to seconds. Most recently I worked on a production AI agent runtime — PostgreSQL schema design including attribute-level access control, a gRPC model gateway with stateless scaling, and performance optimization.

Throughout all of it, the foundation has been backend engineering — FastAPI, SQL, caching, reliability — and I lean on AI tools heavily to ship faster. That's exactly why this role appeals to me: it's scalable FastAPI APIs, solid SQL, and AI-assisted backend development, which is precisely how I already work. And I'm an immediate joiner."

→ **If they probe the Nokia story:** "What specifically did you architect in NFTRACE?"
Honest answer: "The core design decision was separating test scenarios from validation logic — before, they were coupled, so changing validation risked breaking unrelated tests. I designed the FastAPI lifecycle endpoints (start/stop/merge/validate) as the control API, Redis-backed session caching to isolate parallel test runs from each other, and SQLite for persisting node execution history. That decoupling is where the 50% time saving came from."

→ **If they probe the Brane story:** "You were there two months — what did you actually build?"
Honest answer: "Two focused months. I worked on the PostgreSQL schema design — the identity tables, the RBAC permission model with junction tables for the many-to-many agent relationships, and the execution sibling tables. I also hardened the LLM response parser and helped build the verifier-with-retry loop. I contributed to a production runtime in a specific area; I didn't build the entire platform."

# THE REFRAME (the most likely curveball)

**Q. Your resume says 'AI Engineer,' but this is strictly a backend role. Why the switch?**
"It's a fair question, and the short answer is it's not really a switch. All of that AI work sits *on top of* backend engineering — every one of those projects is fundamentally FastAPI services, PostgreSQL schemas, async pipelines, caching, and API integration. The 'AI' part is mostly integrating an LLM as just another backend service — with the same concerns I'd give any dependency: validation, retries, caching, fallbacks. So I think of myself as a backend engineer first, and AI is two things to me: a service I integrate, and a tool I use to code faster. That second part is exactly what your job description asks for."

**Q. How do you actually use AI tools in your work?**
"I use Claude daily as a kind of pair programmer — for scaffolding FastAPI endpoints, writing test suites, generating SQL, and debugging. It roughly doubles my throughput on boilerplate, which frees me to focus on design and edge cases. But I'm disciplined about it: I review every line it produces, I never paste secrets or sensitive code into it, and I treat it as an assistant, not an authority. It speeds me up; it doesn't replace my judgment."

# CLASSIC BEHAVIORAL (have one solid story each)

**Q. Tell me about your most challenging project.**
*(Use the NFTRACE or Brane story from PROJECT_SCRIPTS.md — they double as behavioral answers.)*

**Q. Tell me about a time you improved performance.**
"In the 5G remediation pipeline, engineers were spending hours manually analyzing packet logs. I built a pipeline that recognized errors semantically against a vector database and cached frequent ones in Redis — which took resolution from hours to seconds, with about 70% of routine errors auto-resolved and a 55% drop in mean-time-to-resolution. The Redis caching specifically was what made repeat failures near-instant."

→ **If they probe:** "How did Pyshark actually extract the error information?"
Honest answer: "Pyshark is a Python wrapper over tshark's protocol dissectors. You iterate over packets in a capture file and access protocol fields as attributes — for example, NAS-5GS message types and cause codes for authentication failures. I extracted those fields to build the error signature that fed into the vector database lookup."

→ **If they probe:** "Was the 70% formally measured?"
Honest answer: "Against the prior manual triage baseline over a sample period — not a controlled experiment, but a consistent and dramatic improvement. I'd say 'roughly 70%' and describe the measurement approach if pressed."

**Q. Tell me about a time something failed or you handled unreliability.**
"When I built an AI-powered keyword generator, the LLM API could occasionally fail or return malformed output. Rather than let that break the tool, I built a regex-based fallback so it stayed up 100% of the time even when the model was down, and I validated every output with a parser before returning it. It taught me to treat any external dependency — even an AI one — as something that *will* fail, and to design for that."

→ **If they probe:** "What did 'malformed output' look like from the LLM?"
Honest answer: "The LLM would sometimes return a keyword structure with missing required fields, or with extra text wrapped around the JSON that the parser couldn't deserialize. The hardening was: strict schema validation on the output, and if validation failed, retry with an error message fed back to the model. The regex fallback was the last line — if the LLM was entirely unavailable, pattern matching on the input still produced valid keywords for the most common cases."

**Q. Tell me about a disagreement or conflict.**
"In code reviews I'd sometimes disagree with an approach. My habit is to focus on the technical reasoning, not the person — I'll lay out the concrete trade-off, like thread-safety or maintainability, and propose an alternative, and we decide based on the merits. Actually, that instinct led me to author code-review checklists that the team adopted as a standard, which improved our code-quality scores by about 25%. It turned individual disagreements into a shared standard."

**Q. Tell me about mentoring or leadership.**
"At Nokia I mentored three mid-level engineers toward senior readiness through regular one-on-one review sessions, and earlier I supervised a team of 19 interns in web development. I also wrote the code-review checklists the team adopted. I enjoy raising the bar for the people around me, not just my own output."

**Q. Tell me about a mistake you made.**
*(Pick a real one. Structure: what happened → what you did → what you changed so it can't recur.)* "Early on, I once shipped something that passed my own testing but missed an edge case another team hit. I owned it, fixed it quickly — and more importantly, that experience is part of why I became such an advocate for review checklists and validation at the boundary. The lasting lesson was to design for the cases I'm *not* thinking of, not just the happy path."

## When You Don't Know (this WILL happen — be ready)

**Q. What do you do when you genuinely don't know an answer?**
"My honest approach: I say 'let me think through that' and reason out loud. That's more valuable to the interviewer than silence — they can see how I approach a problem. If I genuinely don't know, I say 'I haven't worked with that directly, but based on [related thing], I'd approach it by...' and give a real attempt. What I never do is invent a number or a technical fact. If they catch a bluff, it destroys everything else."

## Recovery Scripts (for when it goes wrong in the room)

**If the interviewer corrects you:**
"You're right — I was conflating [X] with [Y]. The correct answer is [Z]." Brief, calm, move on immediately. No over-apologizing.

**If you start answering and realize you're going the wrong direction:**
"Actually, let me reframe — the core point here is [one clear thing]..." Redirect cleanly without drawing attention to the stumble.

**If you go completely blank:**
Say "Let me think through that." [3-5 actual seconds of thinking.] If still blank: "I'd want to think about that more carefully. What I do know is [adjacent concept], which suggests [reasoning]..." Never freeze in silence for more than 5 seconds.

**If they probe something on your resume you can't defend deeply:**
"That [tool/library] was [part of the existing stack / something I've used at the integration level rather than building from scratch]. What I can speak to confidently is [the design decision / the integration pattern / what problem it solved]."

# Golden rules for every behavioral answer
- **Brief.** 60–90 seconds. Land the point and stop.
- **Honest + positive.** Never bad-mouth a past employer or teammate.
- **End on a result or a lesson.** Don't trail off.
- **Pivot hard questions toward enthusiasm for *this* role**, then stop talking — don't over-justify.
